CSP: reading a policy for what it actually blocks

Almost every site I test has a Content-Security-Policy. Far fewer have one that would stop an injected script. The gap between “a CSP is present” and “the CSP is a control” is where the finding lives.

Aligned with OWASP WSTG-CONF-12 · CWE-79 · CWE-693

Open the CSP Policy Auditor

What a CSP is for

CSP is a second layer. It does not remove an XSS bug; it decides whether the injected payload can execute or exfiltrate. A strong policy makes an otherwise trivial injection unexploitable, and a weak one makes the header pure decoration. It is defence in depth, never a substitute for output encoding — that is the framing OWASP uses, and it is the framing I use when a client asks whether the CSP “fixes” the XSS.

Three delivery mechanisms exist: the Content-Security-Policy response header (full feature set, preferred), Content-Security-Policy-Report-Only, and a <meta http-equiv> tag. Only the first one blocks anything. Report-Only records violations and enforces nothing, and a <meta> policy silently cannot express frame-ancestors, sandbox, report-uri or report-to.

The directives that decide the verdict

DirectiveWhat it governsWhat sinks it
script-src Which scripts may execute. The one directive that decides whether XSS is exploitable. 'unsafe-inline', 'unsafe-eval', *, data:, http:, or a bare https:.
default-src Fallback for fetch directives that are absent — including script-src. Absent with no script-src: scripts are unrestricted.
object-src Plugin and legacy embed content. Anything other than 'none', in practice.
base-uri Whether an injected <base> can re-point every relative script URL. Missing. This is the quiet bypass of an otherwise strict policy.
frame-ancestors Who may frame the page. *, or missing — no other directive falls back to it.

Two details the auditor is strict about. 'unsafe-inline' is ignored by browsers when a nonce or hash is also present, so it is reported as information rather than a finding — it is legacy padding, not a hole. And 'strict-dynamic' without a nonce or hash to anchor it has nothing to propagate trust from, which is a real weakness rather than a modern policy.

Allowlists lose; nonces win

Host allowlists fail in ways nobody audits for. A permitted CDN that serves a JSONP endpoint hands an attacker arbitrary execution inside your policy. A wildcard subdomain covers whatever gets taken over next quarter. Every new third party widens the list, and the list is never narrowed again.

A strict policy replaces the list with per-response trust: a fresh random nonce (or a hash) on each script, 'strict-dynamic' so trusted scripts can still load their own dependencies, plus object-src 'none' and base-uri 'none' to close the two classic escapes. Reuse of a nonce across responses defeats the whole scheme — it must be unpredictable and per-response.

Confirm it with the auditor

  1. Open the CSP Policy Auditor and paste a URL you are authorized to test.
  2. Press Audit CSP. It is a read-only GET and it reads the enforced header, not a policy you paste in.
  3. Check the first row: if there is no enforced policy, a Report-Only header is called out separately — that combination is a finding, not a pass.
  4. Read the effective script source. Where both script-src and default-src exist, only the one the browser will apply is scored.
  5. Export the report card. Each check carries the directive value it judged, so a developer can reproduce the verdict.

Severities roll up the way you would expect: one high-severity issue makes the policy high risk, medium makes it medium, everything else low. Where a site sends multiple enforced policies, the auditor combines them conjunctively — a resource must pass all of them, so one strict policy can supply a control another omits. The rules are on the methodology page.

The fix

A strict starting policy, served as a response header:

Content-Security-Policy:
  script-src 'nonce-{random}' 'strict-dynamic' https: 'unsafe-inline';
  object-src 'none';
  base-uri 'none';
  frame-ancestors 'none'

The trailing https: and 'unsafe-inline' are deliberate backwards-compatibility padding: browsers that understand nonces and 'strict-dynamic' ignore both. Roll it out in Report-Only first, watch the violation reports, then move it to the enforcing header — and re-audit, because a policy that never leaves Report-Only has never blocked anything.
