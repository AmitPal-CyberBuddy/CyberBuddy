DNS: the records that stop email spoofing and tampering

A domain's DNS records decide two things an attacker cares about: whether mail can be forged in its name, and whether its answers can be quietly redirected. Reading them is easy; reading them correctly is where the mistakes happen.

Aligned with RFC 7489 (DMARC) · RFC 7208 (SPF) · RFC 6376 (DKIM) · RFC 4033 (DNSSEC) · CWE-290 — authentication bypass by spoofing

Why DNS is an authentication layer

Email is the classic example. When a mailbox receives a message claiming to be from @example.com, the receiving server can look up three things in DNS to decide whether to believe it: the SPF record (which hosts may send), the DKIM key (a cryptographic signature), and the DMARC policy (what to do when neither checks out). None of them make a domain "secure" by themselves — they are identity evidence for a specific claim, which is exactly why their absence maps to CWE-290, authentication bypass by spoofing.

I think of SPF, DKIM and DMARC as one chain: SPF says who may send, DKIM proves who actually did, and DMARC tells the receiver what to do when the first two disagree. A domain with all three present and enforced is much harder to spoof than one with none.

The second risk is the DNS itself. Without DNSSEC, an on-path attacker who can inject answers can redirect the domain's traffic or mail to a host they control, and without CAA, any public certificate authority can issue a certificate for the name.

What each record actually does

SPF is a whitelist of senders, published as a TXT record that starts v=spf1 and ends in a qualifier. The qualifier is the part that matters most: -all means "reject everyone else", ~all means "treat everyone else with suspicion", ?all is neutral and barely a policy, and +all authorizes any host — which is worse than having no record at all.

DMARC lives at _dmarc.example.com and tells receivers what to do with mail that fails SPF and DKIM alignment. Its p= tag sets the main policy: none only observes, quarantine diverts, and reject discards. Full coverage also needs pct=100 (the default) and must not weaken subdomains with sp=none. A domain with SPF but p=none is still spoofable; it has just agreed to watch it happen.

DKIM signs mail with a key published under a selector — selector._domainkey.example.com. A tool can only probe the selectors it knows, so a "no DKIM found" result is a hint, never a proof that no key exists.

DNSSEC signs the zone so answers can be verified end to end. A DS record at the parent chains the domain toward a trust anchor; the analyzer requires that delegation signal together with a DNSKEY at the apex before awarding credit.

CAA restricts which certificate authorities may issue for the name. Absent, any public CA may issue; present, a mis-issued certificate is easier to catch.

Reading the grade

RecordWeightWhat earns full marks
DMARC20One policy with p=quarantine or p=reject, pct=100, and no sp=none gap.
SPF15Exactly one record with -all (or ~all), within the 10-lookup budget.
DKIM10A key on the common selectors the tool probes.
DNSSEC10DS at the parent and DNSKEY at the apex.
Name servers10At least two authoritative name servers.
CAA5The nearest inherited or exact CAA set contains an issue policy that names (or forbids) CAs.

A domain that does not handle email is not penalized for missing SPF, DMARC or DKIM — the analyzer labels those checks informational instead. The full scoring contract is documented on the methodology page.
